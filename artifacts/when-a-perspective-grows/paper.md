# When a Perspective Grows

## Experience, learning and the warrant for comparison

Richard George Davis · Final author release · Version 2.6 · 5 November 2026

This final author release supersedes the earlier public v2.4 working paper, which remains preserved in the version history. It is not a journal-accepted manuscript and has not been peer reviewed. Critical responses are welcome, particularly on whether the proposed standard adds to existing measurement theory and what could justify its assumptions about experiential comparison.

### Abstract

When a perspective grows, what has been learned about experience, and what warrants that claim? Learning can change an experience and the procedures through which it is described. Stable reports therefore need not establish stable experiential relations, while changed reports need not establish changed experience. This paper brings established concerns from introspective calibration, longitudinal measurement and response-shift research into objective phenomenology. Its contribution is a critical synthesis with worked examples, not a new validation method. The central distinction is between specifying a common phenomenal target and establishing what happens to it. If measurements at both stages are warranted against that target, their results can support a local endpoint comparison without a prior assumption of complete phenomenal identity. Where the target or its measurement remains unsupported, statistical alignment cannot supply the missing interpretation. A finite construction illustrates the difference between ordinal and metric identification; a conditional model shows how specified rival explanations could be discriminated if its assumptions were justified. A worked reading of a category-learning experiment identifies a real discrimination result, a response rule insufficient to explain it, and the additional warrant a phenomenal conclusion would require. The resulting account permits partial understanding through either preservation or change, while limiting each conclusion to the relation and occasions the evidence supports. Appendix A develops the related formal clarification, distinguishing ruling out candidate characterisations from adding properties uniformly guaranteed of their instances; it specifies another sense in which understanding may or may not grow.

## The argument in ordinary terms

Learning can change both what we experience and how we explain it. Someone who learns about wine may begin noticing acidity; someone who learns a colour distinction may start sorting familiar shades differently. A more detailed answer afterwards does not, by itself, tell us whether the experience changed, the description improved, or both happened together.

The problem is similar to comparing measurements made with an instrument whose settings may have changed. Here, however, the person doing the comparing is also the person who has learned. We cannot simply read off a change in experience from a change in the answer. Nor can we infer that experience stayed the same because the answer did. The instrument analogy concerns the interpretation of evidence; it does not imply that a person is a passive measuring device or that experience is directly readable on a scale. Unlike a separate instrument and object, attention and classification may help shape the experience being compared.

The organising question is: what has actually been learned when a perspective grows? To answer it in a particular case, the paper first asks what would make a comparison trustworthy. We need to say what is being compared, establish what each answer tells us about it, and keep the conclusion within those limits. We might have good evidence that one shade still looks closer to a reference shade than another does, while lacking evidence that the exact differences feel the same or that the whole experience is unchanged. Throughout the paper, “phenomenal” means concerning what an experience is like for the person having it; “warrant” means the evidence and reasons supporting a claim.

This matters for understanding both other people and our own earlier experiences. A perspective might grow through a new experience, a better grasp of a familiar experience, or an additional warranted fact. These achievements can occur together without being interchangeable. Learning can make a perspective richer without making every earlier comparison useless; a richer description need not establish a new experiential fact. Section 2.1 develops these distinctions, and Appendix A examines one of them precisely. Neither part supplies a way to recover an earlier experience in full.

Imagine three colour patches viewed under controlled conditions before and after a course in colour discrimination or naming. One patch, r, is the reference; x and y are the alternatives. The question is which alternative looks more similar to r. This is a question about appearance, rather than which patch has the same name or comes from the same physical surface. Suppose that, before the course, x looks closer to r and the person selects x. The labels are placeholders: no particular real-world colour ordering is assumed.

The distinctions can be kept together in this example:

| Element | What it means in the colour comparison |
|---|---|
| Target | The question being investigated: whether x looks more similar to r than y does, under the stated conditions. |
| Phenomenal relation | The experienced ordering at the occasion in question, which may or may not be accurately reflected in the answer. |
| Response process | How the answer is produced: for example, comparing appearances, applying learned categories, or a mixture. |
| Warrant | The evidence and reasons for interpreting the answers as measurements of the common target and drawing the joint conclusion. |

Keeping the patches physically controlled helps track the stimuli. It does not settle the phenomenal relation. Equally, changing an instruction can affect more than the response process: attention or appearance may change too.

A common target means that we ask the same sufficiently specified question at both stages; it does not mean that the answer, or the experience, must remain unchanged. The target and the response process are distinguished by their roles in the inquiry, not assumed to be causally separate parts of a person.

The argument proceeds through three questions: what are we comparing, what does the evidence let us conclude, and what kind of understanding has increased? Sections 2–4 establish the target and the problems of interpreting reports across learning. Section 5 separates an ordering from an exact geometry; section 6 asks how evidence could distinguish continuity from change, applies the distinctions to a published colour-learning experiment, then extends the question to wine, memory and earlier records. Appendix A returns to the third question: even when a list of possibilities becomes shorter, what additional fact follows?

## 1. The learner in the measurement

Suppose someone learns a word for a feeling she has previously struggled to explain. She begins using it. Later, asked whether her experience has changed, she says that it has become more distinct.

The German word *Fernweh*, for longing for distant places, gives this possibility a concrete form.[26](https://www.duden.de/rechtschreibung/Fernweh) Someone who learns it may gain a useful name for a familiar feeling or begin noticing a pattern she previously left undifferentiated. Whether the experience itself changes is a further question. Needing a phrase rather than a single word did not, by itself, make the feeling unavailable to her.

Her report of greater distinctness could describe several developments. The feeling might have changed. She might now attend to a feature she previously overlooked. She might classify familiar episodes differently, or have acquired a more convenient way to report them. Learning could produce several of these changes together.

Negro already identifies a central difficulty here. In discussing learning and expertise, he distinguishes changed experience from changed judgement and stresses how difficult it is to separate them.[9, §3.2, pp. 14–15](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-025-05103-6) The ambiguity is not a discovery of this paper. His discussion challenges a defence of metaphysical structuralism—the view that an experience’s complete character is determined by its relations to other experiences. My concern is the narrower inference from measurements before and after learning to the preservation of a specified experiential relation. The question is what would warrant that inference without assuming an answer to the ambiguity he identifies.

We should also ask what has happened to the comparison itself. The person making it is no longer in quite the same position as before. The vocabulary, distinctions and standards she uses to answer may be among the things she has learned. We cannot assume that learning acts only on the object of measurement while politely leaving the measuring procedure alone.

This problem bears on a larger philosophical question. If we can describe relations among experiences, perhaps we can understand something about another perspective without having its experiences. But a perspective need not stay fixed while we investigate it. A person may acquire expertise or a genuinely new experiential capacity. How can we tell whether the relations we previously described still characterise what that person experiences?

The general problem is established territory. Longitudinal measurement asks whether observations at different times support a common interpretation. Response-shift research addresses changes in how people assess themselves: King-Kallimanis, Oort and Garst require a consistent factor interpretation before assessing change, while Vanier and colleagues explicitly distinguish the target from its measurement at two occasions.[15, §2.5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10182-010-0129-y); [16, revised-model section](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-021-02846-w) This literature already concerns interpretation as well as statistical adjustment.

The inference from a statistical pattern to its subject matter also requires care. Protzko directly challenges the claim that measurement invariance suffices to establish that the same thing is being measured.[17, abstract and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1177/00131644241282982) Applying that concern to experience does not turn it into a new general principle.

The contribution here is a critical synthesis for objective phenomenology: distinguish the phenomenal target, the evidence for attributing it at each stage, and the strength of the resulting comparison. Once the target is comparably specified and the measurements are warranted at both stages, their results can support a limited claim of preservation or change. The account does not demand a further proof of experiential identity before that comparison can begin. The argument develops two consequences: uncertainty about a response process or metric can leave a weaker, jointly warranted ordering intact, and change in complete character does not alone defeat every partial comparison. These consequences depend on a justified phenomenal interpretation; they cannot be obtained from formal alignment alone. Its worked examples clarify what weaker and stronger claims require; they do not supply a validated experimental method.

The requirement concerns one route to partial understanding. Understanding can also consist in discovering that an experience has changed. The question here is what earns the more specific claim that something about it has survived.

## 2. A relation can describe experience without exhausting it

In [*What Is It Like to Be a Bat?*](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914), Nagel distinguishes learning about a creature from grasping its experience. Knowing how echolocation works does not supply the bat's point of view. A description of function or physiology can leave out what the experience is like for its subject.[1, pp. 438–445](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914)

Nagel allows rough or partial conceptions, while noting that even a partial conception of bat experience requires taking up its point of view at least roughly. He later proposes objective phenomenology: descriptions of aspects of experience intelligible to beings unable to undergo it. He identifies structural features as a possible starting point.[1, p. 442 n. 8; pp. 449–450](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914) The project therefore contains a constructive question as well as a limit. What could a partial description retain?

Consider the judgement that one colour experience feels more similar to a second than to a third. If accurate, it characterises something about how those experiences compare. It need not specify everything about any of them. Lee defends this possibility, distinguishing genuinely phenomenal structural facts from trivial structural truths that do not characterise what an experience is like.[2, §§4–5.3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-022-00576-0)

There is a further distinction between describing an experienced relation and making that description intelligible from every point of view. Lee distinguishes purely structural concepts from concepts with non-structural components.[2, §5.2, “Impure Structure”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-022-00576-0) A colour comparison tied to familiar appearances is not automatically a purely structural fact in his sense. Even a well-supported longitudinal comparison would leave a further question: what can a reader without those experiential capacities understand from its description? The present argument concerns a condition on the evidential use of structural descriptions; it does not establish that a particular description achieves Nagel's cross-perspective goal.

The qualification “if accurate” carries an empirical burden. A relation's suitability as a description of experience does not establish that a particular task measures it. A matrix of judgements could reflect resemblance of appearances, shared names, expectations about objects, or a mixture. The fact that a person is conscious while making the judgement does not decide which of these the matrix captures.

Two questions consequently need separate answers. What would make the relation phenomenal? What warrants attributing that relation to the measured episodes? The first concerns what the proposed fact describes. The second concerns our evidence for saying that it holds. Confusing them lets a definition perform work that belongs to investigation.

This distinction does not require a metaphysics in which relations constitute complete phenomenal character. A critic of that metaphysics can still accept that some experienced relations are genuine. Conversely, someone who holds that a quality is determined by its entire relational structure must still explain how an experimental measure reaches the relevant structure. The measurement problem remains on either view.[3, Box 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2024.06.007)

### 2.1 Different senses of a growing perspective

A perspective can grow in several senses. A person might acquire a new kind of experience, become better at understanding an experience already familiar to her, or acquire an additional warranted fact about it. These developments can occur together, but one does not establish the others. Being able to describe a feeling more fluently is different from having a new feeling; grasping an existing fact more fully is different from knowing an additional fact. Learning a new belief about an experience also need not change the phenomenal concept through which it is thought about. Neither improved task performance nor the elimination of a candidate, by itself, settles whether a concept has changed or only beliefs associated with its use. No identity criterion for phenomenal concepts is supplied here.

Cath distinguishes knowing further truths about possible experiences from having better experiential access to the same coarse-grained answers.[25, §3.3](https://doi.org/10.1080/00048402.2018.1433697) This gives us a reason to ask what has improved before counting the improvement.[^cath-degrees] The distinction also connects the present paper with the separate clarification *Phenomenal Precision and the Separation of Possibilities*.[23] That note asks whether eliminating candidate accounts of an experience must add a property shared by every remaining instance. Appendix A supplies the formal example, condition and possible repairs without requiring the reader to consult that manuscript.

Cath’s distinction helps organise the whole inquiry: we should identify the achievement before deciding how to establish it. The main paper asks what evidence supports a comparison of experience; Appendix A asks what additional consequence follows when candidate characterisations are eliminated. Both concern what a claimed improvement entitles us to say. They remain different questions: a valid formal implication does not establish that a person uses that representation, and an improved measurement does not automatically increase every kind of phenomenal knowledge.

[^cath-degrees]: Cath’s later discussion connects degrees of knowing what an experience is like with degrees of imaginative and recognitional ability, while leaving open whether improved knowledge explains those abilities or is grounded in them.[30, §5.4](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009323758) He considers Lee’s account of phenomenal concepts as one possible account of the relevant abilities (§4.5). For the present inquiry, these competences are another achievement to distinguish from a change in experience: their improvement does not by itself decide whether experience has changed or what warrants a comparison across learning.

## 3. What agreement among methods can establish

Reeves, Amano and Foster compared judgements of material appearance, hue and saturation, and the origin of changes under altered illumination. Their constancy results differed by task.[6, abstract and General Method](https://doi.org/10.3758/PP.70.2.219) This does not mean that one set of answers must be meaningless. Judging the persistence of a surface and judging the persistence of its hue ask different questions.

This is a useful starting point for the terminology above. The task specifies what the observer is asked to judge; it does not by itself reveal the process used to answer or warrant an interpretation of that answer. A difference between tasks can reflect different targets rather than a failure of measurement. Agreement raises the complementary question: do the methods reach the intended relation, or do they share another route to the answer?

Isaac offers a positive account of how structural inquiry can target experience. Phenomenological reflection helps specify what an experiment asks about, while convergence among models derived through different methods can support their interpretation. He also acknowledges imperfect convergence, disputes about phenomenal metrics and the difficulty of moving from group averages to an individual's experience.[4, §5, pp. 19–21; conclusion, p. 22](https://doi.org/10.33735/phimisci.2025.11773)

This gives the inquiry a starting point without making introspection infallible. A narrowly specified appearance judgement can be evidence even when it is open to error. Aldé argues that structuralist accounts themselves rely on introspected similarity and therefore need specific reasons for rejecting particular introspective judgements.[5, §5, “On the reliability of introspection”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-026-05562-5) That argument supports consistency in the treatment of evidence. It does not certify every report or guarantee that a judgement remains reliable after learning.

The shared-process objection concerns what several methods might share. Suppose a person produces the same ordering through verbal ratings, matching and an action task. These could all depend on one learned comparison process. Agreement would then tell us something about the stability of that process. Whether it tracks felt similarity would remain a further question.

Convergence is therefore informative only in relation to the alternatives it helps discriminate. If a category-based account predicts the same pattern as an appearance-based account, another task producing that pattern adds little against that rival. Evidence becomes more useful where the accounts predict different results and the difference survives controls for the processes under comparison. Two measures with different names may still make the same mistake. This objection does not depend on the act of measurement changing experience; it concerns agreement that fails to distinguish the relevant explanations. Task-induced experiential change is a separate possibility considered in section 6.

The lesson is demanding but limited. A phenomenal interpretation should be supported by a specified target and by evidence that addresses plausible alternatives. It cannot be inferred merely from successful alignment. A task disagreement, in turn, must be interpreted before it is counted as a failure to measure experience. The tasks may be measuring different aspects of it.

## 4. Why learning requires renewed warrant

Now consider the same person at two stages. Before learning, a task has reasonable support as a measure of a particular appearance relation. Afterwards, the person gives the same answers. What follows?

A stable result alone does not identify what happened. Learning may have changed the experiential relation, the procedure used to judge it, or both. The reverse also holds: a changed response could reflect a changed procedure applied to an unchanged relation. We need evidence for interpreting the answers at both stages as measurements of the target under comparison. That evidence can include justified transfer of an earlier calibration or fresh validation of the later procedure.

Here, preservation means that the specified relation holds at the compared stages. Evidence from those stages alone does not establish that it remained unchanged at every intervening moment.

### 4.1 Colour: the same patches, a different comparison

Colour makes the need to specify the target especially clear. The light reaching the eye, the differences someone can discriminate, the categories she uses, the speed of her decisions, her memory for a patch and the way its colour appears are different subjects of inquiry. Learning two names where she previously used one could change her sorting or descriptions without establishing a new range of colour experience or sensitivity to a new range of light. Conversely, failure to establish a change in appearance would not establish that appearance stayed unchanged.

Goldstone’s categorisation experiments provide a concrete precedent for this distinction. Training affected later same/different discrimination, with effects depending on the dimensions and category assignments. Goldstone also stresses that those judgements involve memory and attention.[29, pp. 179, 197–199] These findings establish changes in discrimination performance under the tested conditions; they do not by themselves settle whether a particular experienced resemblance changed. The running three-patch comparison is a hypothetical illustration, not a reconstruction of those experiments. Section 6.3 separately works through what Goldstone’s fourth experiment does and does not establish.

Return to the three patches introduced at the outset. Before the course, x looks closer to r and the person selects x. What would the same answer establish afterwards?

A schematic model makes the ambiguity explicit. Let a reported value at time t depend on a relation d_t, a response function f_t and error:

$$
Y_t=f_t(d_t)+\varepsilon_t.
$$

This is an illustrative decomposition, not a fitted model of colour vision. The response function stands for the relevant way the comparison becomes an answer. If both d_t and f_t are unconstrained, observing Y_t does not identify them separately. Explaining a stable report by a stable experience while leaving the response function unspecified simply selects the answer the investigation was meant to test.

Two possibilities could produce the same post-learning answer. In the first, the appearance ordering remains and the subject continues comparing appearances. In the second, y now feels closer to r, but a newly learned category procedure groups x with r and returns the old answer. A verbal rating and an action could agree if both used that procedure. This second possibility requires an item- or category-dependent process; it cannot be produced by a single strictly increasing rescaling of the reversed appearance ordering. The example stipulates two accounts of the same reports. It does not claim that category learning actually produces this reversal.

The alternatives agree about the observations so far. Section 6 asks what further result could favour one over the other under constraints that can themselves be questioned.

The demand is not that f_t remain numerically identical. A supported account of its change could justify comparison of the underlying relations. Familiar longitudinal methods already allow some measurement parameters to vary while retaining grounds for specified comparisons. Widaman, Ferrer and Conger discuss longitudinal factorial invariance and partial invariance; their metric framework supplies a precedent, not a requirement to impose an exact metric on every ordinal claim.[18, abstract; “Full Versus Partial Factorial Invariance”](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-8606.2009.00110.x)

Three continuities need separating. The physical patch may remain the same; the reported similarity ranking may remain the same; and the experienced relation that ranking is intended to measure may remain the same. Preserving the first does not establish the other two. Preserving the reported ranking also leaves open changes in the magnitude of the experienced differences, in comparisons with newly available experiences, or in the procedure producing the report. If the target is only the old ordinal comparison, some of those changes are compatible with its preservation. If the target includes those additional features, the same evidence is insufficient. The claim must specify what counts as the relation being preserved. Tracking a patch or a sound across sessions gives the investigation a way to compare encounters without assuming that its appearance stayed fixed. It allows competing accounts of experiential continuity and change to be investigated without giving either the conclusion in advance.

## 5. What an ordering cannot establish

Suppose the interpretation problem has been answered well enough to support an experienced ordering. There is still a second question: how strong a description does that evidence warrant? The next example addresses that question; it does not establish the phenomenal interpretation assumed for the comparison.

The degree of preservation claimed must also match the measurement. Knowing which of two comparisons is closer is different from knowing the numerical distance of either. This is the distinction between ordinal evidence, which supports an ordering, and metric evidence, which supports numerical distances. An ordering may be warranted where an exact geometry is not.

If a single response function is strictly increasing over the comparisons at issue, it preserves their order even when its scale changes. Its values do not thereby identify exact distances. If response mappings vary across items or tasks, even this simple ordering argument needs further justification. With noisy choices, the relevant order must be inferred under an explicit model rather than read straight from individual answers.

Victor, Aguilar and Waraich develop methods for analysing similarity judgements while limiting metric assumptions. Their framework relates repeated triadic choices to structural constraints, with stated assumptions and limits on what can be inferred.[7, Introduction, Theory and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.46298/mna.12457) It supplies measurement prior art for taking ordinal evidence seriously. It does not establish that every resulting relation is phenomenal.

An elementary construction shows why this distinction changes a claim about identification. Take three points in Euclidean space:

$$
a=(0,0,0),\qquad b=(1,0,0),\qquad c=(3,0,0).
$$

Their distances are 1, 2 and 3 for the pairs ab, bc and ac. Retain them and add:

$$
n=(0,2,0),\qquad m=(0,-2,0),\qquad z=(0,1,3).
$$

Among assignments of the three labelled old points to three distinct points in the six-point set, exactly one preserves their specified distances. The only pair at distance 1 is a,b. With a and b in that order, c completes the required distances 2 from b and 3 from a; reversing them gives no completion. But if we require only the ordering ab < bc < ac, there are sixteen ordered placements. For instance, mapping (a,b,c) to (b,a,n) gives the corresponding distances 1, 2 and √5. Their order is right; their metric is different.

The example also preserves the distinction between retaining old relations and identifying additions through old references. The distances between a, b and c remain unchanged. Both n and m have distances (2, √5, √13) to those old points, while z distinguishes them at √10 and √18. These new–old profiles are comparisons available in the enlarged set; they were not part of the earlier measurements between a, b and c.

This is stipulated geometry. It shows a logical possibility and the difference between two standards of identification. The count concerns this finite construction; it is not an impossibility theorem for every use of ordinal data or for richer data under additional assumptions. It provides no data about actual experiences. In particular, evidence supporting only ordinal comparisons cannot inherit the unique placement established under the stronger metric assumptions. An independently justified temporal correspondence might select counterparts, but it would be additional evidence.

The placement problem should be distinguished from comparison with already tracked labels. Even when a, b and c are identified across occasions, their distances could be (1, 2, 3) at one occasion and (1, 2, √5) at another, with the same ordering ab < bc < ac. The second configuration is realised by a=(1,0), b=(0,0) and c=(0,2). Thus fixing correspondence removes one uncertainty without converting ordinal evidence into evidence of metric preservation. The six-point construction asks the additional question of which placements the specified structure identifies. Neither example establishes that experiential distances actually changed.

This matters for objective phenomenology because one can overstate an account without making a mistake in its mathematics. The mistake occurs when a formally established result is attached to evidence that does not warrant its premises. A precise account of an ordering can be more informative than an exact-looking geometry whose interpretation has not been earned.

## 6. Earning a local claim of continuity

The geometry shows why a warranted ordering need not identify an exact metric. We now return to the earlier evidential problem: what could warrant that ordering at both stages? This section moves from the conditions for an endpoint comparison to a hypothetical discriminator, a worked learning study and retrospective evidence. The examples show how different evidence warrants different conclusions.

Begin with a common target: for example, whether the appearance of stimulus x feels closer to the appearance of r than y does, on a specified dimension and under stated conditions. The design must justify tracking the relevant items and asking the same comparison question at each stage. Tracking stimuli can serve this local purpose without assuming that their appearances stayed unchanged. Other questions, especially those involving newly acquired experiential capacities, may require a different account of correspondence.

Now suppose there is adequate support at each stage for that same ordinal claim. Taken together, those findings support the ordering at both endpoints. This does not require an additional premise that the experiences are numerically identical or wholly alike. Nor does it show that the ordering persisted at every intervening moment. With fallible measurements, the evidence must support the joint conclusion at the claimed confidence; two uncertain findings do not become certain through conjunction.

This gives the comparability requirement a limit. Reliable answers to different or underspecified questions cannot simply be combined. But a justified common target and measurements validated at both stages can supply the needed grounds. Later validation may draw on an earlier calibration or on fresh evidence. The preservation of the specified relation is then a possible conclusion of the comparison, not a premise used to establish its validity.

The relevant alternatives concern how the actual judgement is made. Does the subject compare appearances, apply familiar labels, reconstruct a remembered impression, or infer which stimuli ought to go together? A task can produce stable answers through any of these routes; its interpretation needs evidence appropriate to the distinction under investigation.

A study could estimate a relation using one portion of its evidence and test predictions on held-out comparisons. It could compare a model preserving the old relations with one allowing them to change, while giving both a justified account of the response procedure. The models would need constraints: a response function allowed to absorb every discrepancy would make preservation too easy to protect. Held-out prediction tests whether a model generalises beyond the answers used to estimate it. It does not by itself show that the model tracks appearance rather than a category procedure shared by the estimation and held-out tasks.

The checks should challenge the explanation rather than merely reproduce its preferred result. Changing category cues may help distinguish a naming strategy from another account. Changing response demands may reveal a task-specific procedure. But neither manipulation can be assumed to affect only reporting. It might change attention or experience too. The role of the manipulation must itself be supported; there is no general instruction that turns an intervention into a pure test of judgement.

A learned category need not be external to experience. It could guide a decision, affect attention, alter appearance, or do several of these things. The contrast in the example is therefore between two specified explanations of the answer, not between cognition as such and genuine experience. Equally, a neural similarity measure would add evidence only with an account of what it measures and how its relation to the target survives learning. Calling a measure neural does not discharge that interpretative burden.

Revalidation need not start from nothing. Existing evidence can continue to support parts of the procedure for which the transition supplies no relevant reason for doubt. The demand concerns the parts of the inference that the learning could reasonably affect, and the claim being made about them.

### 6.1 A conditional discriminator

Return to the hypothetical r, x and y comparison. Before learning, x feels closer to r than y does. The two accounts now differ as follows. In the continuity account, that ordering persists. In the reversal account, y has become closer, but a learned category procedure still selects x. Both accounts can explain the repeated post-learning answer on a task governed by that category. To distinguish them, we need a comparison on which their developed explanations make different predictions.

For an illustrative model, suppose a comparison based on appearance selects the option that feels closer with probability 0.9: it gets that comparison right nine times out of ten in the long run. Let a category procedure always select x and supply the answer on a proportion w of trials. On the remaining trials, the answer comes from comparing appearances. These are stipulated routes, not measured components of a real observer. For a task on which w is 1, both accounts predict x on every trial. That task cannot separate them.

Now consider a further task on which the following constraints are warranted. The table specifies the support a real application would need; familiarity controls, instructions or the absence of speech would not establish these conditions on their own.

| Assumption in this model | What the evidence would need to support |
|---|---|
| Appearance comparison selects the closer option with probability 0.9 after learning | A warranted calibration of that accuracy for the relevant comparison; 0.9 is stipulated here, not an observed estimate. |
| The category-route probability w is at most 0.1 on the further task | A constraint on w that is not obtained merely by fitting the target answers to the preferred account. Instructions or the absence of spoken labels alone do not establish this bound. |
| The further task does not reverse the ordering under investigation | An account of task and attention effects adequate to connect its results to the target comparison. |

These are requirements for using the numerical example, not three validated tests. Calibration transfer, task contrasts or other evidence could contribute if their interpretations were supported. There is no requirement that the support be exclusively neural, or that experience be isolated from every cognitive influence.

Under those conditions, the predicted proportion of x choices is w + (1 − w) × 0.9 in the continuity account, and w + (1 − w) × 0.1 in the reversal account:

| Account | Predicted proportion of x choices on the further task |
|---|---|
| Continuity: x still feels closer to r | 0.90–0.91 |
| Reversal: y now feels closer, with a remaining category route favouring x | 0.10–0.19 |

In ordinary terms, one account predicts x choices on roughly nine trials in ten; the other predicts them on fewer than two in ten. An estimated choice rate near 0.9, with uncertainty small enough to distinguish these ranges, would favour continuity over this reversal account. A rate near 0.1 would favour reversal. A rate around 0.5 would fit neither specified model well; it would prompt revision of the accounts or their constraints. An uncertain estimate spanning both ranges would not discriminate them. Predictions and constraints must be fixed before inspecting the target results, rather than adjusted to protect a preferred account.

The positive result would remain conditional on the validation. If the category route dominated the further task too, both accounts could again predict x. If learning altered the accuracy of appearance comparison, or the further task altered the experience, these particular predictions would lose their warrant. A reversal account with other independently supported response processes could also require a different comparison. The example therefore establishes how evidence could discriminate two constrained accounts, not a general procedure that isolates experience from judgement.

Even a successful comparison would support only this ordering against the specified reversal alternative. It would not establish exact distances, complete phenomenal identity or the absence of every other change. The burden identified by Negro persists wherever the constraints needed to distinguish the accounts lack support. No empirical comparison resolving that burden is completed here.

### 6.2 Which conclusions survive uncertainty?

Uncertainty about the response process need not leave every claim equally unresolved. Suppose the evidence supports several accounts of how answers are produced. They differ about the response procedure or exact experiential distances, but each supports the same appearance ordering at both stages. If the evidence adequately covers the relevant alternatives and warrants their shared phenomenal interpretation, the ordering can remain supported without selecting a unique response account or metric. This is a claim-relative judgement: disagreement that matters to a stronger conclusion need not defeat a weaker conclusion on which the supported accounts agree.

Contrast three outcomes. If the remaining uncertainty concerns only the precise distances or response procedure, retain the warranted ordering and withhold those stronger claims. If a comparably supported account instead reverses the appearance ordering, the evidence does not settle continuity of that ordering. If all the accounts explain the choices but lack grounds for interpreting them as comparisons of appearance, their agreement supports a behavioural result, not the phenomenal conclusion. Agreement among models cannot supply an interpretation none of them has earned.

The relevant accounts are those sustained by the evidence at the claimed level of confidence, not every imaginable explanation and not an arbitrarily selected list. Their coverage and shared interpretation require justification; a neglected, credible reversal account would reopen the conclusion. This does not provide a new statistical test or solve the calibration problem. It explains why the unresolved question must be stated at the level of the claim: exact metric, ordinal relation, response process or whole character. The discriminator in section 6.1 illustrates one way uncertainty could be reduced under justified constraints; it is not a requirement to identify every detail of the process before making any experiential comparison.

A bounded application must identify which uncertainty the available evidence actually reduces. Discovering a change in discrimination can be informative even when a claim about changed appearance remains unresolved. If the further claim is that an ordering persisted while exact experiential distances changed, the metric-change claim needs its own justified metric evidence; ordinal judgements alone cannot establish it. The following case puts these differences to work rather than assuming the required calibration has already been obtained.

### 6.3 A worked application: learning colour categories

Goldstone’s fourth experiment supplies a real learning case to which these distinctions can be applied.[29, Experiment 4, pp. 192–197] Eighty participants were divided equally among four groups. Using sixteen squares varying in brightness and saturation, three groups learned categories based on brightness, saturation or both; the fourth received no categorisation training. All groups then completed the same same–different discrimination task. The assessment below interprets the published design and results; it is not a reanalysis of the data or a reconstruction of the hypothetical three-patch task.

The comparison was between training groups and an untrained control group, not a measurement of each participant’s experienced similarity before and after learning. Goldstone measured discrimination sensitivity using d′, which takes account of both correct “different” responses to different squares and false “different” responses to identical squares. This matters because the groups also differed in their tendency to answer “same”. A difference in raw response frequency should not simply be read as improved discrimination, much less as a change in how far apart the colours feel.

For the groups trained on one dimension, discrimination along that dimension was better than in the control group not only across the learned category boundary but also between different values within a category.[29, pp. 192–194] The within-category finding gives the evidential comparison a specific point. Consider a rule whose only input is whether the correctly assigned category labels match. An identical pair and two different members of the same category both give that rule the same input. The rule therefore cannot, by itself, explain discrimination between those cases. This is an informational limitation of that narrowly specified rule, not a fitted-model rejection reported by Goldstone.

The result does not exclude category-based processing generally. Noisy or graded category representations, confidence about a label, category-guided attention, memory and mixtures with other discrimination processes could retain information within a category. Goldstone explicitly acknowledges memory and attention in same–different judgements.[29, p. 179; General Discussion, p. 197] Excluding the impoverished label-matching rule is consequently a real narrowing of one explanation, not a demonstration that the remaining process directly measures experienced resemblance.

| Claim under consideration | What the evidence permits | Limit or further requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Training is associated with improved discrimination under the tested conditions | The reported sensitivity comparisons with the untrained group, including within-category comparisons | These group results are not measurements of each person’s earlier and later experience. |
| Correct category-label identity alone explains the discriminations | This rule lacks the information needed to distinguish identical from distinct same-category pairs | Richer category, attention, memory and mixed-process accounts remain live. |
| A particular experienced similarity ordering changed or survived | The physical stimuli and task outcomes specify a setting for investigation | Appearance-directed comparisons with a justified common target at both stages, and evidence addressing relevant alternatives to their interpretation. |
| The numerical bounds in section 6.1 hold for these participants | Nothing in this application estimates those parameters | Evidence for the appearance-comparison accuracy, route-frequency bound and task transfer used in that hypothetical. |

The next evidential question is therefore specific: does training change an appearance-directed comparison for these same stimuli, and can the pattern be distinguished from the remaining response accounts? A study pursuing the paper’s endpoint claim would need relevant stage-specific evidence, including a pre-learning comparison; this experiment cannot supply it retrospectively. Comparisons within as well as across learned categories would challenge the simple label rule. Contrasts in memory demands, category cues or response instructions could test more developed alternatives, but would need their own interpretation because they may also change attention or appearance. These are proposed discriminating questions, not validated controls or an assurance that the gap will close.

The application therefore retains a published learning effect, identifies an insufficient explanation and leaves the phenomenal conclusion open for stated reasons. It does not recast Goldstone’s evidence as a failure. His broader perceptual-sensitivity claim and this paper’s narrower claim about a particular experienced relation require different arguments.

### 6.4 Wine, memory and the earlier point of view

The endpoint question concerns evidence at two stages; the worked study shows what a post-training group comparison leaves open. Retrospective comparison asks how present recollection and an earlier record can contribute when the earlier episode is no longer available for inspection.

Consider a hypothetical learner returning to a familiar wine after a tasting course. Assume for this illustration that the wine presented and the tasting conditions are unchanged. She now describes its acidity and texture, where an earlier note said only “pleasant”. Perhaps those features were present but went unnamed; perhaps her attention changed; perhaps the taste itself is different for her now. The brief old note cannot settle which account is right. Its silence about acidity is not evidence enough that acidity was absent from her experience.

Memory adds another difficulty to retrospective comparisons. The person who learned can still refer to an earlier experience and may remember it accurately. But the present recollection is not the earlier episode made available for a second inspection. The subject now compares a current experience with a recollection of a past one. If learning has changed the concepts or standards used in that comparison, a confident retrospective report cannot, on its own, tell us which differences belong to the earlier experience and which belong to its present interpretation.

This distinguishes reference from reinspection. It does not follow that the subject has become incapable of understanding her past, or that every new experience must alter every old memory. The narrower difficulty is evidential: the earlier episode cannot be retested as an episode occurring then. Repeating its stimulus produces a later encounter, while remembering it produces present evidence about the earlier one. Neither makes the temporal distance disappear.

Consider a listener returning to a familiar song. A lyric once understood as describing another person's wrongdoing may now prompt consideration of the listener's own responsibility. The changed reading illustrates a possible change in interpretation; it does not establish that either moral assessment is correct. Nor does remembering the earlier response reproduce the earlier experience for direct reinspection. The song, its present interpretation and the remembered earlier interpretation remain distinct objects of comparison.

Autobiographical-memory experiments offer a bounded example. Butler and colleagues repeatedly cued nominated memories and manipulated retrieval perspective. Retrieval history affected later observer-perspective and visual-imagery ratings; the corresponding interaction for first-person ratings was not significant, and a later perspective-switch task did not repeat the full phenomenology questionnaire. Repeated first-person retrieval also helped preserve later availability of visual details, but this does not independently verify their accuracy or the original experience.[20, Experiment 2, pp. 245–246] The study therefore shows that some reports and available imagery can depend on retrieval history; it does not establish that the original experience changed or that access to its phenomenal character improved.

A third-person image need not mean that the event has been disowned: Libby and Eibach connect viewpoint to whether attention is on the event’s experience or its coherence with a current self-concept.[21, abstract] Nor should a vivid sense of revisiting be treated as a settled test of episodic memory; De Brigard challenges autonoetic consciousness as a validated and necessary marker while allowing that recollective phenomenology may be distinctive.[22, §§3–4]

Contemporaneous reports provide a third kind of evidence. A note made before learning may preserve an earlier judgement without preserving the experience itself. Later recollection may help interpret that note; a repeated stimulus may permit a new comparison. A longitudinal account should distinguish these sources and justify how they are connected. Their agreement can support a local conclusion, but it cannot be treated as independent confirmation if the sources share the later classification being investigated.

The new tasting, her present recollection and the old note answer different questions. Tasting again gives her a present experience. Recollection supplies present evidence about the earlier occasion. The note preserves something she reported then. None can simply stand in for the others. If the aim is to compare the experienced acidity at the two occasions, the investigator must justify how these sources bear on that target. If the aim is only to establish that she now uses a richer description, the new vocabulary may suffice. The difference is the claim being made, not whether learning was valuable.

The question is therefore what grounds the particular comparison across the transition. Where learning gives us reason to doubt a measurement or its target interpretation, those doubts need an answer. Where a common target and adequate measurements are already supported at both stages, there is no automatic further obstacle simply because the learner has changed.

## 7. Objections and the remaining limit

The first objection is that the account presupposes access to experience. It starts with appearance judgements, then uses them to validate a method supposedly independent of the first person.

The account does begin with fallible first-person evidence. It does not promise to derive phenomenal facts from evidence stripped of every phenomenal interpretation. A publicly assessable inquiry can use a subject's reports as evidence while investigating their reliability. Objectivity of an account is different from the absence of first-person contributions to its construction. Nagel proposes descriptions intelligible without undergoing the target experience.[1, p. 449](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914) I take this to leave open the use of subjects' evidence in constructing such descriptions; it is not an explicit rule for measurement supplied by his essay.

This starting point remains contestable. Schwitzgebel challenges broad confidence in ordinary introspection, including under seemingly favourable conditions.[8, author's draft](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/Naive070907.htm) His challenge blocks an unrestricted reliability premise. The present account needs a narrower case for the particular comparison, with specific alternatives and defeaters. It cannot respond to introspective error by simply renaming a judgement an observation.

Spener offers a positive approach: introspective judgements may be warranted through their role in explaining possession of introspection-reliant abilities, with feedback from exercising them. The account already allows empirical correction and specifies conditions and contents for which judgements can be trusted.[14, §§3–5, author manuscript](https://doi.org/10.1111/phis.12062) It is not a static account to which the present paper adds revision. Better current competence could support present judgement without, by itself, establishing an earlier relation; this is an application of the calibration problem, not a refutation of Spener.

Pauen argues that mental measurement can improve beyond the accuracy of reports used in its development.[19, §§3–4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-023-09931-0) His replay example makes the proposed source of constraint concrete. Experimenters control when visual stimuli switch, then compare subjective reports with an eye-movement measure. The externally specified switching times provide information not obtained merely by fitting the preferred interpretation to the reports. However, the standard is indirect: a stimulus switch is taken to cause the relevant experiential switch, and serious challenges to that connection require additional evidence. Pauen presents his case studies as proofs of principle, not unquestionable successful calibrations.[19, §4 and §4.2.1] The example supplies a possible form of corrective evidence without requiring a wholly introspection-free starting point. It does not establish that the stimulus–experience connection transfers across colour-category learning, or that Goldstone’s discrimination task measures experienced resemblance. Those are further claims with their own evidential burdens.

The second objection is that section 6 has moved the problem into its assumptions. How could the category route and comparison accuracy be constrained without already knowing which answers reflect experience? This is the strongest limitation of the numerical example in section 6.1. A numerical separation between predictions cannot validate the assumptions that produce it. The example shows the conditional form of an evidential comparison; it does not itself provide evidence for the needed constraints. The worked application in section 6.3 makes progress against one specified response rule but does not establish those numerical bounds or a phenomenal ordering. Where evidence cannot support the required interpretation against the shared-process objection in section 3, the phenomenal conclusion must remain unresolved.

There are two different demands hidden in the request for independent calibration. One is to avoid choosing a response model simply because, under it, the target answers deliver the desired phenomenal conclusion. The other is to require calibration without any first-person evidence or without any uncertainty about cognitive implementation. The first addresses a genuine circularity; the second does not follow from it. A control can constrain a particular rival without establishing a complete cognitive architecture. Goldstone’s identical and distinct same-category pairs illustrate the narrower evidential function, even though they do not settle the appearance question.

For a phenomenal comparison, some support must also bear on why the retained evidence concerns appearance. Repeating an instruction to “compare appearances” cannot provide that support on its own. The argument instead permits fallible first-person evidence to be assessed alongside task-specific checks, while requiring that those checks could count against the preferred interpretation. This is not independent access to experience from outside every phenomenal judgement. Nor is it permission to calibrate a measure by declaring its successful answers veridical. If an equally supported account reverses the target ordering, or the available accounts lack a warranted phenomenal interpretation, that phenomenal verdict remains unearned. The proposed middle position rejects both automatic trust in convergence and a demand for complete process identification before any local claim can be justified.

A third objection comes from phenomenal holism. If a quality's complete character depends on its entire repertoire, acquiring a new capacity could change that complete character. Negro's Novel Experience Argument presses this consequence, and Sebastián questions how divergent structures can ground phenomenal correspondences.[9, §3.2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-025-05103-6); [10, pp. 7–11](https://doi.org/10.33735/phimisci.2025.11819)

The inference from complete-character change to the failure of every partial comparison needs an additional premise. A whole can acquire new relations without every relation among its retained elements changing: in section 5, the old distances remain while the repertoire and the available comparisons expand. If complete character depends on that wider repertoire, its alteration does not by itself entail the alteration of each specified old relation. Conversely, preservation of an old relation would not establish preservation of complete character. This is a logical distinction between the claims, not a geometrical proof about actual experience.

A holist can still challenge whether the supposedly retained relation has the same phenomenal content in the altered repertoire. That objection concerns the common target and its warranted interpretation; matching an abstract pattern cannot answer it. Where the challenge succeeds, the local comparison remains unsupported. Where a common phenomenal relation and its attribution at both stages are warranted without assuming complete-character identity, complete-character change alone is no further defeater. The account therefore neither refutes holism nor solves Sebastián's correspondence problem. It identifies the extra step needed to turn a claim about the whole into a prohibition on a particular partial comparison.

A fourth objection concerns whether the account creates a needless extra burden. If both stages already warrant the same defined ordinal claim, what remains to be established before comparing them? For endpoint preservation of that ordering, nothing beyond an adequate joint evidential basis is automatically required. Section 6 makes this explicit. Common-target justification may be part of the stage-specific validation; it need not be a third independent test. Demanding proof of complete phenomenal identity would change the question and risk assuming the result. The criticism is useful where the target or measurement is underspecified, not as a reason to withhold every local conclusion.

The final objection concerns novelty. Meredith's formal treatment distinguishes forms of measurement and factorial invariance.[11, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294825) His full text was unavailable for this comparison, so no claim about its precise treatment or omission of the present argument follows from that record. Sprangers and Schwartz's account of changed self-evaluation and Oort's proposed response-shift method supply further precedents.[12, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0277-9536(99)00045-3); [13, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16022054/)

The more detailed comparisons introduced in section 1 leave little basis for claiming a new general requirement. King-Kallimanis and colleagues already address common interpretation and changes in measurement; Vanier and colleagues make target and measure distinct; Protzko contests the leap from statistical invariance to substantive measurement. Calling the target phenomenal does not by itself add a principle those approaches lack.

The contribution is to make those established concerns bear on specific decisions in objective phenomenology. Repeated answers alone leave the experienced ordering unsettled; a common target and adequate joint evidence can warrant an endpoint comparison without complete phenomenal identity. Ordinal evidence can support a useful description without inheriting the uniqueness of a metric reconstruction. Where supported rival explanations disagree about the target relation, that comparison remains unresolved. These are different verdicts, not one general warning that measurement is difficult. Their value lies in keeping a warranted partial conclusion available while blocking stronger claims the same evidence cannot support. The worked category-learning case shows how that discipline can retain an empirical result and narrow one explanation while withholding a stronger phenomenal claim. This is a critical synthesis and application, not a new measurement theory, an empirical resolution of the calibration problem or an established claim of historical priority.

## 8. What we can carry forward

When a perspective grows, the first task is to identify the achievement. The person who learns *Fernweh* may gain a useful description; the wine learner may acquire a more differentiated experience; a comparison may establish that an earlier relation has survived or changed. These are possibilities to investigate, not interchangeable meanings of improvement.

The hypothetical three-patch example shows how the answer can remain modest and informative. If a common target and adequate joint evidence support x looking closer to r than y does at both occasions, we can conclude that this ordering holds at both endpoints even if the reporting procedure has changed. That establishes an endpoint ordering, not exact distances, complete phenomenal identity or uninterrupted persistence. If the evidence instead supports reversal, we have learned about a change. If it cannot distinguish the rival accounts, the answer remains open. A useful account must make room for all three outcomes.

There is a further question even when we know that possibilities have been ruled out: what additional fact does that elimination yield? Appendix A shows why a shorter candidate list need not add a property common to every surviving instance. Such growth requires an admitted separator under the uniform-instance rule. The representation may still register other gains, just as better grasp of an existing fact can matter without adding another fact. The point is to describe the gain accurately, not to deny its value.

The two strands therefore answer the organising question at different levels. The main text asks what the evidence warrants saying about experience across learning; the appendix asks what follows from a specified change in possibilities. Neither substitutes for the other. Together they make a growing perspective more intelligible by separating changes in experience, changes in its description and changes in what we are entitled to conclude. Understanding need not recover an earlier experience in full. It can advance by establishing a particular relation, recognising a particular change, or identifying what the available evidence still leaves unresolved.

## Appendix A. When fewer possibilities yield an additional fact

The main paper has asked what evidence earns a comparison across learning. This appendix returns to the question of what has been gained: once possibilities have been ruled out, what additional fact follows?

To connect the questions, an application would first need evidence warranting the elimination and a justified interpretation of the survivors as the relevant phenomenal candidates. Its phenomenal conclusion would then be limited by the consequences that the resulting representation supports. The response-process accounts in section 6.3 are not automatically such candidates: learning that a label rule is insufficient does not itself establish a fact about every remaining possible experience. This appendix supplies the conditional consequence test, not that missing translation. Its narrower question is whether eliminating a candidate characterisation must add a property shared by every instance still permitted. A shorter list and a new common property are different kinds of improvement.

The companion clarification[23] examines a strict-growth claim in Lee’s *Knowing what it’s like*, which is a different paper from his *Objective Phenomenology* discussed in section 2.[24, §§6.1–6.2](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpe.12187) The following reconstruction is limited to properties holding throughout the surviving instance range. Its elementary common-attribute operation is familiar from formal concept analysis; no new general mathematical result is claimed.[27, §1](https://doi.org/10.4204/EPTCS.129.8) It does not assume that these are all the phenomenal facts Lee’s broader account can express.

Lee’s §6.1 represents each phenomenal possibility as a set of total experiences and a concept’s open possibilities as a family of those sets. Section 6.2 makes a universal condition on attributed phenomenal properties necessary and sufficient for knowability through a concept. It also claims strictly more knowable phenomenal facts when one concept rules out everything another rules out and more.[24, §§6.1–6.2](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpe.12187) The formula below is this paper’s reconstruction of that uniform-property test, not notation displayed by Lee. The question is whether strict elimination guarantees strict consequence growth if that test exhausts the facts at issue. Its application to Lee also depends on whether the candidate families are eligible in his intended domain.

### A.1 A shorter list can leave the same common ground

Let a, b and c stand for possible total experiences in a formal model. A candidate characterisation is represented by the experiences it permits as instances. Consider three candidates: E₁={a,b}, E₂={a,c} and E₃={b,c}. Suppose E₁ is the actual candidate and remains open throughout. Initially all three candidates are open; afterwards E₃ has been excluded. The list has become shorter. Yet the total range covered by the surviving candidates is still {a,b,c}: E₁ and E₂ together already cover every instance that E₃ allowed.

The two levels of description can be compared directly:

| What is tracked? | Before elimination | After eliminating E₃ |
|---|---|---|
| Open candidate characterisations | E₁, E₂, E₃ | E₁, E₂ |
| Instances collectively allowed | {a,b,c} | {a,b,c} |
| Uniform instance consequences in the same fixed vocabulary | The original set | The same set |
| Does every surviving candidate permit a? | No | Yes |

The three candidates have equal cardinality, and none contains another, so the example does not depend on treating a determinable and one of its determinates as interchangeable. Equal cardinality does not, however, establish equal phenomenal specificity or make these sets eligible phenomenal candidates.

The intended comparison holds the target property fixed and retains its actual candidate in both families; only the open characterisations differ. It does not treat a change of target as improved understanding. Nor need it describe a learning episode: two concepts can be compared statically. The model concerns property targets represented by instance ranges and does not automatically extend to every interpretation on which the target is a particular experiential episode.

Any property true of every instance covered afterwards was therefore already true of every instance covered before. This conclusion concerns common instance properties, not every possible kind of information. We have ruled out a candidate; we have not thereby acquired a new property shared by all surviving instances. The example is set theory, not an established case of how human phenomenal concepts work.

### A.2 The exact condition

Let T be the instance domain, S a nonempty family of distinct nonempty candidate subsets of T, and Φ a fixed vocabulary of admitted instance predicates, represented by their extensions in T. Define

$$
U(S)=\bigcup_{E\in S}E,\qquad K_{\mathrm{inst}}(S)=\{p\in\Phi:U(S)\subseteq p\}.
$$

Representing predicates by extensions does not identify phenomenal properties with sets or individuate fact contents solely by extension. Distinct contents with the same extension remain distinct; the fixed uniform test gives each the same verdict in an equal-union comparison. The admitted vocabulary also stays fixed. If learning changes which contents are available, that is another possible source of growth. Formal consequence under this rule does not by itself establish concept possession, warrant or a subject’s ability to draw the inference.

For nonempty survivor families A⊊B, with a fixed actual candidate retained where required,

$$
K_{\mathrm{inst}}(B)\subsetneq K_{\mathrm{inst}}(A)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists p\in\Phi\,[U(A)\subseteq p\ \land\ U(B)\nsubseteq p].
$$

In words, eliminating candidates adds a common instance property exactly when an admitted property holds throughout what remains but fails somewhere in the earlier range. Such a property separates the two ranges. Since U(A)⊆U(B), every previous uniform consequence is retained; strict growth requires at least one additional consequence, which is precisely the displayed condition.

There are two different obstacles. The surviving candidates may still cover the same instances, as in A.1. Or their range may shrink while the admitted vocabulary lacks a property that detects the difference. Allowing every subset of T as a predicate makes any proper shrinkage detectable, but that is a mathematical benchmark, not a reason to count every subset as a phenomenal property.

For a finite candidate family C with at least two members, strict growth on every nonempty strict-subfamily comparison holds if and only if each removable candidate E has an admitted predicate true throughout U(C∖{E}) but false somewhere in E. Necessity follows by removing E alone; sufficiency follows by using the predicate for any candidate removed in the comparison at issue. With every subset admitted, this reduces to E having an instance not covered by the other candidates. Pairwise disjointness is sufficient but stronger than necessary. If a fixed actual candidate E* must remain, the condition is required only for E≠E*. If only some transitions are permitted, separation is needed only for those transitions.

### A.3 Three ways to handle the distinction

One response keeps the rule and qualifies the claim: elimination retains uniform consequences, and adds one when a separator exists. A second independently restricts the eligible candidates or transitions so that the required separation is guaranteed. A third admits richer facts about the candidates themselves. These responses alter different parts of the account.

If Lee’s intended phenomenal domain independently guarantees the required separators, the strict-growth claim survives there. The formal example would then identify a condition satisfied by that domain rather than a counterexample to the account. Mutually exclusive answers to which property the target is do not, by themselves, establish that those properties have disjoint instance ranges; a domain restriction must do the relevant work.

For example, both surviving candidates E₁ and E₂ permit a as an instance, while E₃ does not. The claim “every remaining candidate permits a” becomes true after elimination. It is a claim about what candidate properties allow, rather than a property shared by every surviving total experience. No fixed instance predicate can make this distinction when the instance unions are equal. More generally, a candidate-sensitive rule can retain information that the union-based test cannot register. Whether that information is a phenomenal fact available through the focal concept needs a philosophical argument; set inclusion does not supply it.

Lee’s discussion of determinability supplies a limited motivation for considering disjunctive phenomenal properties: “phenomenal red” can refer to a determinable whose precise determinates include phenomenal crimson and phenomenal scarlet.[28, §2.3, pp. 269–271](https://doi.org/10.1215/00318108-8809919) This is distinct from an experience being phenomenally imprecise. It makes a suitably typed disjunctive target worth considering; it does not establish that arbitrary unions are phenomenal properties or that an OR-composed concept inherits candidates by set union.

The companion note also gives a conditional compositional example. Let P and Q be disjoint nonempty instance sets and R=P∪Q. Suppose one constituent concept has candidate family {P}, while another, actually referring to Q, is refined from {P,Q} to {Q}. If inclusive OR combines their possible interpretations by union and the complex inherits exactly those possibilities, its candidate family changes from {P,R} to {R}, while its instance union remains R. Both complex concepts target R. The candidate family has changed in a way the uniform instance test cannot detect.

Lee separately discusses whether two concepts’ possibility families overlap, as a basis for what can be ruled out about their target identity.[24, §6.2](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpe.12187) Relative to the first constituent considered separately as the fixed anchor, with family {P}, the complex changes from overlap to disjointness between candidate identities. P and R still overlap as instance sets. Conditional on Lee’s stated rule, the refined complex considered together with the fixed anchor rules out their having the same target; the earlier complex does not rule it out on that candidate-family basis. The resulting contrast is between two concepts, not automatically an additional fact yielded by the refined concept alone. The example assumes the OR rule, the exact inherited candidate families and their phenomenal eligibility. None is independently established here. If the thinker already recognises the second constituent as exactly Q, or already treats the operands as phenomenally distinct, the P branch is unavailable and the example fails. The conditional example is compatible with a richer Lee account rather than a refutation of it.

### A.4 How this bears on a growing perspective

The connection can be seen by asking what each representation leaves out. In the colour example, the same answer can fit different accounts of the experienced relation and the response process. In A.1, the same combined instance range can come from different families of candidate characterisations. Looking only at the answer in the first case, or only at the union in the second, can conceal a distinction relevant to the question being asked. This is an explanatory parallel, not one result proved twice: the first is an evidential identification problem; the second follows exactly from the stipulated consequence rule.

The common lesson is to specify what kind of improvement a comparison can establish. A shorter list of candidates, a larger stock of common instance facts, better grasp of an unchanged fact and better evidence for a temporal comparison are different achievements. Each may matter. The separation result identifies a condition on one formal inference; it does not validate a measure, establish a real learning mechanism or show that the main paper has isolated experience from judgement. The main paper’s evidential requirements likewise do not prove that the candidate families in this appendix are admissible. Keeping these questions distinct lets the two arguments inform one another without making either do work it has not earned.
